# Moat Types

A moat is a *structural* reason this company's economics survive competition. Seven durable types, each with a test and a failure mode. Many companies have no real moat — name that bluntly instead of inventing one.

## 1. Brand

**Definition**: customers pay more, or prefer, for reasons tied to identity/trust rather than product specs.

- **Test**: if a commodity clone appeared at 30% lower price with identical specs, would loyal customers still pay the premium? By how much?
- **Proof signal**: gross margin premium vs category average that persists ≥5 years.
- **Failure mode**: brand is only as good as the last scandal. Luxury bands lose it to counterfeits + dilution; consumer brands lose it to Gen-Z indifference.
- **Examples**: Hermès, Apple (iPhone mix), LVMH, Moutai. **Not** brand moats (despite being well-known): Starbucks (has one but weaker than people assume), Coca-Cola (strong in legacy markets, weak in new).

## 2. Network Effect

**Definition**: each additional user makes the product more valuable to existing users.

- **Variants**: direct (messaging apps), indirect (two-sided marketplaces), data (more users → better model → better product).
- **Test**: would a new competitor with identical product at zero price still fail to take users? If yes, the network effect is real.
- **Proof signal**: low/zero marketing spend per user, high retention, competitive attempts visibly struggle.
- **Failure mode**: local networks (Tinder, delivery) can fragment; indirect network effects collapse when one side goes multihomes.
- **Examples**: WeChat, Meta family, Visa/Mastercard, LinkedIn in professional networking, Airbnb (asymmetric — stronger for hosts than guests).

## 3. Cost Advantage

**Definition**: structurally lower unit cost than competitors, not from temporary optimization.

- **Sources**: scale economies (shared below), preferential input access, geographic advantage, process know-how, vertical integration.
- **Test**: if competitors tried to match your price, would they lose money? At what volume does their math get worse?
- **Proof signal**: best-in-class operating margin at the largest scale without sacrificing product quality.
- **Failure mode**: cost leadership built on subsidies / labor arbitrage / regulatory gaps is fragile.
- **Examples**: Walmart (logistics), Geico (direct distribution), Costco (buying power + low overhead).

## 4. Switching Cost

**Definition**: leaving is painful even when a better alternative exists.

- **Variants**: procedural (data migration, retraining), financial (sunk investments), relational (embedded in workflow with partners).
- **Test**: how many quarters of pain would a customer endure to save N% on price?
- **Proof signal**: very low churn despite mediocre product, high price increases accepted without flight.
- **Failure mode**: tech shifts reset switching costs (cloud reset on-prem lock-in; AI may reset SaaS lock-in).
- **Examples**: Oracle DB / EBS (legendary), SAP, Salesforce (moderate), Bloomberg Terminal (social + procedural), ICP in the Apple ecosystem.

## 5. Scale Economies

**Definition**: costs per unit drop as volume rises, and the leader's advantage widens rather than shrinks.

- **Test**: at 2× scale, does your per-unit cost drop materially? If yes, size is a weapon.
- **Proof signal**: market share leader with structurally better margins, and the gap widens over time.
- **Failure mode**: scale in a declining or mature market is a liability (think Kodak scale in film).
- **Examples**: TSMC (leading-edge node R&D amortized over larger volume), AWS (data centers), Amazon retail (fulfillment density).

## 6. Counter-Positioning

**Definition**: from Hamilton Helmer's *7 Powers*. Incumbent cannot copy the challenger's model without cannibalizing its own profits.

- **Test**: if the incumbent replicated your move, would they have to sacrifice their existing business? How much?
- **Proof signal**: incumbent *knows* about the threat, has the resources to respond, and still doesn't.
- **Failure mode**: eventually the incumbent swallows the loss and counters. The question is whether the challenger reaches escape velocity first.
- **Examples**: Netflix vs Blockbuster (physical-store P&L trapped Blockbuster), Dell direct vs channel-distribution PCs, 拼多多 vs Alibaba (low-end offering would dilute Alibaba's brand + merchant mix).

## 7. Process Power

**Definition**: internal know-how, built over years, that competitors can't replicate even with a recipe.

- **Test**: if you handed a complete set of SOPs to a competitor, could they match your output in 24 months? If no, process power is real.
- **Proof signal**: consistent quality/yield/defect differential that persists across generations of leadership.
- **Failure mode**: process power is tacit — if the institutional memory walks out or gets disrupted, it decays faster than it looks.
- **Examples**: Toyota Production System, TSMC node yield, ASML lithography assembly.

## Also Worth Naming (Not Always Durable)

- **Regulatory / license moat** — banking, telecom, utilities. Real but politically dependent.
- **Patent / IP moat** — time-limited; strongest in pharma, weaker in software.
- **Capital moat** — being able to outspend competitors on loss leaders. Only durable if you also have one of the 7 above underneath; otherwise it's just burning money.

## What to do in the report

1. Declare the *primary* moat type with confidence, plus any secondary.
2. Apply the **test** for that type — show evidence, don't just assert.
3. State a **failure mode** — what would kill this moat in 3–5 years.
4. If no real moat exists, say so. "Operating in a commodity market with temporary execution advantage" is a valid finding and often more honest than inventing a moat.
